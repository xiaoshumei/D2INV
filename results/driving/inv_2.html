55 Years of American Driving

Miles Traveled vs. Gas Prices (1956-2010)

Explainer: Watch how the nation’s appetite for the road collided with volatile pump prices—and see which way the needle really moved.

How did the long cheap-fuel era shape America’s driving habit up to the first oil shock?

Between 1956 and 1973, vehicle-miles climbed steadily from 3,675 B to 6,154 B while gas prices drifted down from $2.38 to $1.90 per gallon (2021-corrected dollars), creating a classic cheap-fuel boom that rewarded every extra mile.

What happened to driving distance and price the moment oil-supply security cracked?

The 1973-74 embargo flipped the script: miles dropped 3.4 % in 1974 and prices spiked 23 % to $2.34—still the fastest single-year cost jump in the series—revealing immediate elasticity once pain at the pump arrived.

Did the second oil crisis finally force Americans to curb mileage growth?

From 1978 to 1981, miles inched up only 4 % while gas rocketed from $2.23 to $3.30, producing the steepest price-to-miles ratio (3.2×) of any three-year window and marking the first sustained conservation period.

Fast-forward to the 2000s

See if drivers really forgot the sting of high prices.

Does driving still react to price spikes once memories of crisis fade?

After 1986’s price collapse to $1.76, miles resumed growth; yet sharp price spikes in 2005 and 2008 again trimmed mileage—2008’s $3.31 peak coincided with a rare year-over-year decline, proving price sensitivity never went away.

In the very long run, did higher gas prices meaningfully slow the growth of driving?

Over the full 55-year span, cumulative miles expanded 2.7× while real gas prices rose only 10 %, yielding an almost vertical long-run supply curve—implying that, barring acute shocks, Americans choose mobility almost regardless of gradual price creep.
