Which specific disasters and years produced the highest death tolls in recorded history?

Drought dominates the deadliest single-year events: China’s 1931 flood (3.7 M deaths) is the only non-drought entry in the top-5; the other four are droughts—1928 China (3 M), 1920 China (2.5 M), 1943 India (1.9 M) and 1942 India (1.5 M). These five years together account for ≈16.6 M deaths, about 25 % of all 1900-2017 disaster fatalities.

How has the nature of disaster mortality shifted before vs after the mid-20th-century?

Before 1950 drought and epidemic spikes drove extreme annual variability (σ ≈ 643 k deaths); after 1950 variability fell but remained substantial (σ ≈ 154 k) while earthquake, flood and storm fatalities became the main contributors to remaining risk.

Do earthquake fatalities show a declining trend or episodic extreme spikes?

Earthquake deaths cluster in three seismic mega-years—1923 Great Kantō (152 k), 1976 Tangshan (277 k) and 2004 Sumatra–Andaman (227 k)—each producing sharp jumps versus adjacent decades, illustrating episodic extreme risk rather than a declining trend.

Is the global drop in drought deaths uniform or do localized high-mortality events still occur?

While drought killed > 12 M people inside China and India between 1900-1965, post-1980 global drought mortality stayed below 1,000 in 75 % of years, yet single-country episodes (1983 Ethiopia, 2010 Somalia) still briefly drive five-digit tolls, demonstrating that vulnerability remains locally acute even as global frequency falls.

Has the relationship between disaster mortality and societal exposure changed in the 21st century?

The 2000s mark a mortality transition: total disaster deaths average 84 k yr⁻¹ versus 53 k yr⁻¹ in the 1990s, while economic losses soared, suggesting adaptation (early-warning, building codes) is saving fewer lives than exposure of assets rises, a decoupling that accelerates after 2004.
